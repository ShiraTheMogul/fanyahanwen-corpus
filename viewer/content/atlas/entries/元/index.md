## Overview

The Great Yuan (大元) was a khanate led by the Mongol Empire and a dynasty of China. It was founded by Kublai Khan 忽必烈, the fifth Great Khan 大可汗, and lasted from 5th May 1260 to around 1368, when it fell apart with the fall of Dadu 元大都. It then split into the Northern Yuan and Ming dynasty, the former of which continued until 1635. 

## History

Before the arrival of the Mongol Empire, China was occupied by the Han Chinese Song dynasty. At the time, the Song had experienced an extreme conflict with the neighbouring Jin dynasty and retreated to to the south following repeated military defeats. Initially allied with the Mongol Empire, Emperor Gong of Song 宋恭帝 broke a treaty with Ögedei Khan 窩闊台 by capturing the former imperial capitals of Kaifeng 開封, Luoyang 洛陽, and Chang'an 長安 as the Jin 晉 and Western Xia 西夏 were conquered. Kublai Khan 忽必烈 responded by crushing the Song through conquering Dai Viet 大越 and attacking from multiple sides, encircling them completely and displacing of millions of people. This culminated in the Battle of Yamen where the Prime Minister of Song, advisor Lu Xiufu 陸秀夫, held the 7-year old Zhao Bing 宋帝昺 in his arms, and jumped off a cliff, marking an end to the Southern Song.

Upon taking control of the Chinese region, the Mongol Empire dismantled the Song state and imposed a Mongol ruling class and four-tier race-privilege system. This same pattern occurred with the Persians, Koreans, and Kievan Rus'. Had Yelü Chucai not convinced the Mongols to tax rather than destroy the Han Chinese, it is possible that there could have been a genocide. Japan would not interact with the Mongols, whereas Goryeo would given their tributary status, enraging Kublai Khan and leading to him sending a letter in Classical Chinese demanding relations be established. 

## Culture

The Yuan dynasty attempted to co-exist with the native Chinese culture, with the Phags-pa script commonly being used to write Mongolian, Tibetan, and Chinese at once. The Mongol colonisers were not overly concerned with Chinese culture, possibly due to fears of power-sharing with the conquered masses, employing a tiered system of privilege: Mongols at the top, then Semu, then Han Chinese, then any individuals from Southern Song. There was also a strong stigma against race-mixing: A Semu person could not marry a Han Chinese person. One's ethnicity, therefore, had a significant influence on their future. Ergo, very few tradiitonal Chinese scholar-officials managed to reach the posts they once did, which would be decried by the Yongle Emperor 永樂帝 for discrimination after the Yuan dynasty's eventual demise. Many scholar-officials who served in government were forced to leave and become doctors, teachers, and more. Literary Chinese therefore took a backseat in the now-Mongol-led government, but would rise elsewhere in the form of vernacular literature as scholars continued writing outside of the aristocracy. Homosexuality was common, but severely suppressed, being illegal in the Great Yassa and decried by Confucianists. Despite this, enforcement was famously lax in ways not unlike anti-sodomy laws in Florence, leading to a visible and flourishing queer culture. 

The Mongolian people worshipped a sky deity called Tengri 撐犁, who was also worshipped by the Xiongnu and Xianbei people. This religion is centred on a personal relationship with the deity, has no central body of literature, and is therefore quite decentralised. Tengrism is still an extant religion in Mongolia, but is endangered and not embraced by the regions it exists in. It experienced a small resurgence following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, especially in Türkiye. Mongol women were often quite free and accompanied their husbands on campaigns in a way not unlike the Shang dynasty, which clashed with the subservience expected in Neo-Confucian society; the assertiveness of Mongol women can be seen in plays, and is a significant shift from Song literature. The Great Yassa instituted significant rights for women, including the prohibition of kidnapping, adultery, and sale of women, and authorised their entry into the military. All of a man's children, including those illegitimate, were legitmate heirs. 

Kublai Khan initially moved away from the Confucian institutions run by the Han Chinese after conquering Southern Song. Therefore, the imperial examination commonly seen in traditional Chinese culture was not used. Despite this, he attempted to sinicize and integrate with the Han Chinese by supporting Daoism and Buddhism, as well as cultural practices such as painting. Buyantu Khan 普顏篤汗, Emperor Renzong of Yuan 元仁宗, revived the imperial examination as part of a sinification campaign with the encouragement of the Confucian academic Li Meng 李孟, his tutor. Jayaatu Khan 札牙篤汗, aka Emperor Wenzong of Yuan 元文宗, would continue integration practices as well. 

Poetry moved towards Qu 曲 poetry, to the point poetry of the era would be called Yuan Qu 元曲. Poetry about death and destruction, called sangluan 喪亂, became omnipresent, with Yuan Haowen 元好問 and Shang Ting 商挺 being notable practitioners of the genre. Sanqu 散曲, a subgenre of Qu poetry, skyrocketed in popularity for its humour and exasperation with the disenfranchisement of the old literati. Zaju 雜劇, a type of Chinese opera, was inspired in part by Sanqu poetry. One example of a Zaju playwright would be Bai Renfu 白仁甫, whose 16 plays are great sources of early Mandarin Chinese prose. Each of these genres are marked for their dramatic expressions, sombre tone, and greater emphasis on contemporary spoken language.

## Use of Literary Chinese

Literary Chinese took a backseat in the Mongol-led government given the de-emphasis of Confucian education in government. The standardised Middle Chinese seen in the Song dynasty Guangyun 廣韻 ceased to function, accelerating its split into topolects such as Mandarin Chinese that are seen today. These two changes mean that there is much more vernacular literature seen in this period, something not covered by the corpus. Many of the Chinese classics were translated into Mongolian, but little to no Mongolian literature was translated into the language, a political choice. When Kublai Khan took power, he wrote his ascession and use of the Great Yuan name in Classical Chinese, and would use the language to communicate with Japan and Goryeo. 

Given the self-imposed cultural isolation put in place by the Mongol colonisers, it is somewhat rare to find Literary Chinese used by them. However, some writing can be found: Bukha Timur 布哈特穆爾, of the Uyghur Kingdom of Qocho 高昌, wrote ci 詞 poetry filled with references to the natural world around him. His poetry shows reception of traditional Chinese norms. One is a zhuzhi ci 竹枝詞, a type of yuefu 樂府, echoing Jiangnan 江南 landscapes. His other poem, a gongci 宫詞, discusses palace seculsion, referencing the Han Dynasty Jianzhang Hall 建章 of Chang'an.
